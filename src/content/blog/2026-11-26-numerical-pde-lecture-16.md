This is my summary of the sixteenth lecture in my numerical PDE course. I write the ideas in my own words and check them with a small experiment.

[Lecture 15](/blog/2026-11-24-numerical-pde-lecture-15/) ended with the minmod slope. Minmod stops the wiggles next to a jump, but it also clips smooth peaks. This lecture asks for more: keep the jumps clean **and** keep the peaks. It adds two better limiters (MC and superbee), writes the same idea in the language of fluxes, and gives a simple test for TVD (Harten's theorem). At the end it moves to the first nonlinear equation, Burgers' equation.

Notation as before: $\Delta x$ is the cell size, $\Delta t$ is the time step, $Q^n_i$ is the average over cell $i$ at time $t_n$, the model problem is $q_t + aq_x = 0$ with $a > 0$, and $\nu = a\Delta t/\Delta x$ with $0 \le \nu \le 1$. The slope method from [Lecture 15, section 5](/blog/2026-11-24-numerical-pde-lecture-15/) is

$$
Q^{n+1}_i = Q^n_i - \nu\big(Q^n_i - Q^n_{i-1}\big) - \frac{\nu}{2}(1 - \nu)\,\Delta x\,\big(\sigma^n_i - \sigma^n_{i-1}\big).
$$

## 1. Overshoot is not a bug in one scheme

In Lecture 15 every linear slope (Fromm, Beam–Warming, Lax–Wendroff) made overshoot or undershoot next to a jump. This is not bad luck. When we approximate a discontinuous function with smooth pieces, overshoot and undershoot appear by nature. Unless the scheme does something special, there is no way around it.

The special thing is a **limiter**: a slope that depends on the data. Near a jump it becomes small, and in smooth parts it stays close to a second-order slope. Because the slope depends on the data, the scheme is nonlinear, even for a linear PDE.

## 2. High order and high resolution

Two words are easy to mix up.

- **High order** is about smooth solutions. A scheme is $p$-th order if the error behaves like $\Delta x^p$ when the solution is smooth. Some schemes go to fifth order and beyond.
- **High resolution** is about solutions with jumps. A high-resolution scheme resolves a discontinuity well: the jump stays sharp and does not wiggle.

Lax–Wendroff and Beam–Warming are much more accurate than upwind for smooth solutions, but they fail near a discontinuity. They can wiggle even for smooth data with steep parts. A limiter is the fix: it turns a high-order scheme into a high-resolution one.

## 3. The MC limiter: compare three slopes

Minmod compares two slopes, the one from the left and the one from the right. The **monotonized central (MC)** limiter also brings in the centered slope of Fromm, which was the best linear slope in Lecture 15. It compares three slopes:

$$
\sigma_i = \operatorname{minmod}\!\left(\frac{Q_{i+1} - Q_{i-1}}{2\Delta x},\ 2\,\frac{Q_i - Q_{i-1}}{\Delta x},\ 2\,\frac{Q_{i+1} - Q_i}{\Delta x}\right).
$$

Here minmod of three numbers is the one with the smallest size if all three have the same sign, and $0$ otherwise. The same limiter is often written in two steps, $\operatorname{minmod}\big(\text{central},\ \operatorname{minmod}(2\cdot\text{left}, 2\cdot\text{right})\big)$. This is only notation: the two forms are equal (experiment, part 1).

**Why the factor 2?** One answer is scale: the centered slope uses two cells and the one-sided slopes use one. But the main reason is which slope wins.

- In a smooth region the two one-sided slopes are both close to the centered slope. Twice a one-sided slope is then about twice the centered slope, so it is larger, and minmod picks the **centered slope**. So MC is Fromm (second order) wherever the data are smooth.
- A one-sided slope wins only when it is smaller than **half** the centered slope. That happens next to a jump or at a peak. There the limiter falls back to the small one-sided slope (or to $0$ at a peak, because of the sign rule).

So MC presses down much less than minmod, at most by a factor of 2, but it is still TVD (section 7).

## 4. Superbee: press down as little as possible

The **superbee** limiter goes further. It builds two candidates with minmod, then takes the **larger** one:

$$
\sigma^{(1)}_i = \operatorname{minmod}\!\left(\frac{Q_{i+1} - Q_i}{\Delta x},\ 2\,\frac{Q_i - Q_{i-1}}{\Delta x}\right),\quad
\sigma^{(2)}_i = \operatorname{minmod}\!\left(2\,\frac{Q_{i+1} - Q_i}{\Delta x},\ \frac{Q_i - Q_{i-1}}{\Delta x}\right),\quad
\sigma_i = \operatorname{maxmod}\big(\sigma^{(1)}_i, \sigma^{(2)}_i\big).
$$

Maxmod is the opposite of minmod: if the two numbers have the same sign, it picks the one with the **larger** size, otherwise $0$. Among these limiters, minmod presses the slopes down the most and superbee the least. Superbee keeps jumps very sharp. The experiment (section 10) shows its cost: it tends to make smooth peaks flat on top, like a small box.

There are many more limiters. Each way to choose $\sigma$ has a name, and a search finds dozens of them.

## 5. Total variation of a function

Lecture 15 defined total variation for cell averages, $TV(Q) = \sum_i |Q_i - Q_{i-1}|$. The same idea works for a function $q(x)$: take any points $\xi_0 < \xi_1 < \dots < \xi_N$, add up $|q(\xi_j) - q(\xi_{j-1})|$, and take the supremum over all such choices. If $q$ is differentiable, this is $TV(q) = \int |q'(x)|\,dx$. For schemes we only need the grid version.

## 6. The same schemes as flux limiters

A finite volume scheme is a choice of numerical flux ([Lecture 13](/blog/2026-11-17-numerical-pde-lecture-13/)). So we can also describe limiting with fluxes. Slope limiters and flux limiters are two languages for the same thing. Slopes are easier to picture; fluxes are more natural for finite volume design.

A **flux limiter** mixes a low-order flux $\mathcal F_L$ and a high-order flux $\mathcal F_H$ at each wall:

$$
F_{i-1/2} = \mathcal F_L + \Phi_{i-1/2}\,\big(\mathcal F_H - \mathcal F_L\big).
$$

With $\Phi = 0$ we get the low-order flux, and with $\Phi = 1$ the high-order flux. For $0 \le \Phi \le 1$ it is a convex combination of the two. Think of $\Phi$ as a door on the wall: it decides how much of the high-order correction may pass. The hard part is to design $\Phi$.

Take upwind as $\mathcal F_L = aQ_{i-1}$ and Lax–Wendroff as $\mathcal F_H$ ([Lecture 14, section 7](/blog/2026-11-19-numerical-pde-lecture-14/)). The Lax–Wendroff flux can be written as $aQ_{i-1} + \tfrac{a}{2}(1 - \nu)(Q_i - Q_{i-1})$. With the jump $\Delta Q_{i-1/2} = Q_i - Q_{i-1}$ (a plain difference, not divided by $\Delta x$), the mixed flux is

$$
F_{i-1/2} = aQ_{i-1} + \frac{a}{2}(1 - \nu)\,\Phi_{i-1/2}\,\Delta Q_{i-1/2}.
$$

Compare it with the slope flux from Lecture 15, $aQ_{i-1} + \tfrac{a}{2}(1 - \nu)\,\Delta x\,\sigma_{i-1}$. They are the same if $\Delta x\,\sigma_{i-1} = \Phi_{i-1/2}\,\Delta Q_{i-1/2}$. So the "limited jump" $\Phi\,\Delta Q$ of the flux language is exactly $\Delta x$ times the slope of the upwind cell.

For a general sign of $a$, write $a^+ = \max(a, 0)$ and $a^- = \min(a, 0)$:

$$
F_{i-1/2} = a^- Q_i + a^+ Q_{i-1} + \frac{|a|}{2}\left(1 - \left|\frac{a\Delta t}{\Delta x}\right|\right)\delta_{i-1/2},\qquad \delta_{i-1/2} = \Phi_{i-1/2}\,\Delta Q_{i-1/2}.
$$

Only one of $a^+$ and $a^-$ is nonzero, so the flux takes the upwind value from the left when $a > 0$ and from the right when $a < 0$. (Books use different symbols for $a^\pm$, so check the definition.) For linear advection we know the direction in advance. For nonlinear equations the direction depends on the solution, and that is what makes them harder (section 9).

## 7. The smoothness ratio θ and the function φ(θ)

How should $\Phi$ be chosen? Look at the ratio of two neighboring jumps. For $a > 0$ the upwind neighbor of wall $i-1/2$ is wall $i-3/2$:

$$
\theta_{i-1/2} = \frac{\Delta Q_{i-3/2}}{\Delta Q_{i-1/2}} = \frac{Q_{i-1} - Q_{i-2}}{Q_i - Q_{i-1}}.
$$

If the data are smooth, two neighboring jumps are almost equal, so $\theta \approx 1$. Near a discontinuity one jump is large and the other is not, so $\theta$ is far from $1$. At a peak the two jumps have different signs, so $\theta < 0$. So $\theta$ is a **trouble indicator**. The scheme computes it from the data at every step and finds the trouble by itself.

Then we set $\Phi_{i-1/2} = \phi(\theta_{i-1/2})$ for some function $\phi$. The linear schemes of Lecture 15 fit into this form, and we can read off $\phi$ from section 6. With $\Delta x\,\sigma_{i-1} = \phi(\theta)\,\Delta Q_{i-1/2}$:

| slope in cell $i-1$ | $\Delta x\,\sigma_{i-1}$ | $\phi(\theta)$ |
|---|---|---|
| zero (upwind) | $0$ | $0$ |
| downwind (Lax–Wendroff) | $\Delta Q_{i-1/2}$ | $1$ |
| upwind (Beam–Warming) | $\Delta Q_{i-3/2} = \theta\,\Delta Q_{i-1/2}$ | $\theta$ |
| centered (Fromm) | $\tfrac12(\Delta Q_{i-3/2} + \Delta Q_{i-1/2})$ | $\tfrac12(1 + \theta)$ |

The limiters fit the same way, because their slopes scale with the data. For example, minmod gives $\Delta x\,\sigma_{i-1} = \operatorname{minmod}(\theta, 1)\,\Delta Q_{i-1/2}$, so $\phi(\theta) = \operatorname{minmod}(1, \theta)$. MC and superbee have similar $\phi$ formulas. The formulas are not the point. The point is that catching a discontinuity needs some real work, and $\phi(\theta)$ is the place where that work happens. I checked numerically that the slope form and the flux form give the same update for all seven methods (part 1).

## 8. Harten's theorem

Every scheme above can be written, for $a > 0$, as

$$
Q^{n+1}_i = Q^n_i - \nu\big(Q^n_i - Q^n_{i-1}\big) - \frac{\nu}{2}(1 - \nu)\Big[\phi(\theta_{i+1/2})\big(Q^n_{i+1} - Q^n_i\big) - \phi(\theta_{i-1/2})\big(Q^n_i - Q^n_{i-1}\big)\Big].
$$

When is such a scheme TVD? Harten's theorem gives a simple test.

**Theorem (Harten).** Write a one-step scheme as

$$
Q^{n+1}_i = Q^n_i - C^n_{i-1}\big(Q^n_i - Q^n_{i-1}\big) + D^n_i\big(Q^n_{i+1} - Q^n_i\big),
$$

where the coefficients may depend on the data. If for every $i$

$$
C^n_{i-1} \ge 0, \qquad D^n_i \ge 0, \qquad C^n_i + D^n_i \le 1,
$$

then $TV(Q^{n+1}) \le TV(Q^n)$.

The lecture did not prove it. Here are two uses (my own working).

**Upwind.** $Q^{n+1}_i = Q_i - \nu(Q_i - Q_{i-1})$, so $C = \nu$ and $D = 0$. The conditions are $0 \le \nu \le 1$: the CFL condition again.

**Flux limiters.** In the formula above, use $Q_{i+1} - Q_i = (Q_i - Q_{i-1})/\theta_{i+1/2}$. Then everything is a multiple of $Q_i - Q_{i-1}$, so $D = 0$ and

$$
C_{i-1} = \nu\left[1 + \frac{1 - \nu}{2}\left(\frac{\phi(\theta_{i+1/2})}{\theta_{i+1/2}} - \phi(\theta_{i-1/2})\right)\right].
$$

Suppose $\phi(\theta) = 0$ for $\theta \le 0$, and

$$
0 \le \phi(\theta) \le \min(2\theta,\ 2) \quad \text{for } \theta > 0.
$$

Then both $\phi(\theta)/\theta$ and $\phi(\theta)$ lie in $[0, 2]$, so their difference lies in $[-2, 2]$, and

$$
\nu^2 \le C_{i-1} \le \nu(2 - \nu).
$$

For $0 \le \nu \le 1$ this is inside $[0, 1]$, so the scheme is TVD. This region in the $(\theta, \phi)$ plane is the "TVD region". It also explains the factor 2 in MC and superbee: they go right up to the edges $\phi = 2\theta$ and $\phi = 2$. To be second order for smooth data, $\phi$ should also pass through $\phi(1) = 1$, the Lax–Wendroff value. All three limiters do.

Lax–Wendroff has $\phi \equiv 1$. When $\theta$ is close to $0$, the term $1/\theta$ is huge, so $C$ can be far above $1$ or below $0$. Beam–Warming has $\phi = \theta$, which leaves the region for $\theta > 2$. That is why the linear second-order schemes are not TVD. Note that Harten's conditions are sufficient, not necessary. A $C$ outside $[0, 1]$ does not prove that $TV$ grows in that step. But the experiments in Lecture 15 and below show that it does grow in practice.

## 9. A first nonlinear equation: Burgers

So far the speed $a$ was a constant. A nonlinear conservation law is

$$
u_t + f(u)_x = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u_t + f'(u)\,u_x = 0
$$

for smooth solutions. Now $f'(u)$ plays the role of the speed. We can play the same game as for linear advection ([Lecture 14, section 5](/blog/2026-11-19-numerical-pde-lecture-14/)): define a curve by

$$
\dot X(t) = f'\big(u(X(t), t)\big), \qquad X(0) = x_0.
$$

Along this curve, $\frac{d}{dt}u(X(t), t) = u_t + f'(u)\,u_x = 0$, so $u$ is constant. Then the speed $f'(u)$ is also constant along the curve, and the characteristic is a **straight line**:

$$
X(t) = x_0 + f'\big(u_0(x_0)\big)\,t.
$$

The new difficulty is that the speed depends on the unknown $u$. We do not even know its sign in advance, so we do not know which side is upwind.

The simplest example is **Burgers' equation**, with $f(u) = u^2/2$ and $f'(u) = u$:

$$
u_t + \Big(\frac{u^2}{2}\Big)_x = 0, \qquad X(t) = x_0 + u_0(x_0)\,t.
$$

Each point of the initial data moves with its own value as speed. In the $(x, t)$ plane the characteristic from $x_0$ has slope $1/u_0(x_0)$: large values give lines that lean far to the right, and $u_0 = 0$ gives a vertical line. We can sketch all characteristics without solving anything. Burgers' equation also has exact solutions, which makes it a standard test problem.

**Characteristics can cross.** A small example of my own: let $u_0 = 1$ for $x \le 0$, let it fall linearly from $1$ to $0$ on $[0, 1]$, and let $u_0 = 0$ for $x \ge 1$. The characteristic from $x_0 \in [0, 1]$ is

$$
X(t) = x_0 + (1 - x_0)\,t.
$$

At $t = 1$ every one of these lines reaches $X = 1$. Points behind move faster than points in front, so the back catches up with the front. At the meeting point, one characteristic brings the value $1$ and another brings the value $0$, so $u$ cannot have one value there. A discontinuity forms. In physics it is called a **shock**. This happens even though the data were continuous. So for nonlinear laws, jumps are not a special case, and the limiters of this lecture are really needed. Computing such solutions is hard, and the plan is to copy the ideas from the linear case. What happens after the shock forms is the topic of the next lecture.

## 10. Experiment

I added MC and superbee to the slope code of Lecture 15 and tested the flux form and Harten's coefficients, for $q_t + q_x = 0$ on the periodic interval $[0, 1)$. The script uses only the Python standard library.

**Part 1: the different forms agree.** The nested form and the three-argument form of the MC minmod agree exactly on $200{,}000$ random triples. On random data ($64$ cells, $\nu = 0.3$ and $0.8$), the slope form of section 6 and the flux form with $\theta$ and $\phi(\theta)$ agree to at most $3.3 \times 10^{-16}$ for all seven methods (upwind, Fromm, Beam–Warming, Lax–Wendroff, minmod, MC, superbee).

**Part 2: Harten's coefficients.** I drew about $100{,}000$ random pairs $(\theta_{i+1/2}, \theta_{i-1/2})$ in $[-3, 3]$ and computed $C_{i-1}$ with $\nu = 0.7$. The bound from section 8 is $[\nu^2, \nu(2 - \nu)] = [0.49, 0.91]$.

| method | smallest $C$ | largest $C$ | share outside $[0, 1]$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| upwind | $0.70$ | $0.70$ | $0\%$ |
| minmod | $0.595$ | $0.805$ | $0\%$ |
| MC | $0.49$ | $0.91$ | $0\%$ |
| superbee | $0.49$ | $0.91$ | $0\%$ |
| Beam–Warming | $0.49$ | $1.12$ | $19.2\%$ |
| Fromm | $-49.2$ | $52.7$ | $4.6\%$ |
| Lax–Wendroff | $-99.3$ | $104.4$ | $7.2\%$ |

MC and superbee reach both ends of the bound exactly, because they touch both edges of the TVD region. Minmod stays well inside. The three linear schemes leave $[0, 1]$; Lax–Wendroff and Fromm go very far when $\theta$ is near $0$.

**Part 3: smooth data.** As in Lecture 15, I moved $\sin 2\pi x$ (as exact cell averages) once around with $\nu \approx 0.6$. Orders are $\log_2$ of the error ratio between the last two grids.

But it is not TVD (part 4).

- **Minmod** presses too hard. After five periods the bump is down to $0.83$ and the box edges are twice as wide as with MC.

But on the bump its error is four times larger than MC's after one period ($0.0028$ against $0.0007$). The peak value is high ($0.99$), but the shape is wrong: in the chart the top of the superbee bump is flat and its sides are too steep. Superbee squares off smooth peaks, as section 4 warned.

## Summary

1. Overshoot near a jump is natural for smooth approximations. To avoid it, a scheme needs a data-dependent slope, a **limiter**, and so it becomes nonlinear.
2. **High order** is accuracy for smooth solutions. **High resolution** is a sharp, clean jump. Limiters give the second without losing the first in smooth parts.
3. **MC** takes the smallest of the centered slope and twice each one-sided slope. It is Fromm in smooth parts and falls back near jumps and peaks. **Superbee** takes the larger of two minmod candidates and presses the least.
4. A slope limiter is also a **flux limiter**: $F = \mathcal F_L + \Phi(\mathcal F_H - \mathcal F_L)$ with $\Phi = \phi(\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the ratio of neighboring jumps, a trouble indicator. Upwind, Lax–Wendroff, Beam–Warming and Fromm are $\phi = 0, 1, \theta, (1 + \theta)/2$.
5. **Harten's theorem**: $C \ge 0$, $D \ge 0$, $C + D \le 1$ implies TVD. For flux limiters this gives the region $0 \le \phi(\theta) \le \min(2\theta, 2)$.
6. For a nonlinear law $u_t + f(u)_x = 0$, characteristics are straight lines with speed $f'(u_0(x_0))$. For **Burgers' equation** they can cross, and a **shock** forms even from smooth data.
